GLOSSARY

Connected Discourse
"Connected speech is more than just a string of individual target segments joined together in series, since each segment is liable to influence the segments that surround it..." (Howard, Wells, & Local, 2008)[cite: 11]. In linguistics, connected speech or connected discourse is a continuous sequence of sounds forming utterances or conversations in spoken language[cite: 11]. Analysis shows sound changes affecting linguistic units traditionally described as phrases, words, lexemes, morphemes, syllables, phonemes, or phones[cite: 11].
Coherence
It is the unifying element in good writing[cite: 11]. It refers to the unity created between the ideas, sentences, paragraphs, and sections of a piece of writing[cite: 11]. Coherence gives writing its flow and makes reading easier to follow as ideas appear in a natural, logical way[cite: 11]. In linguistics, it is the property of unity stemming from the links among underlying ideas and logical organization[cite: 11].
Cohesion
The use of repetition, transitional expressions, and other devices (cohesive cues) to guide readers and show how the parts of a composition relate to one another[cite: 11]. In linguistics, cohesion is the property of unity in a written text stemming from links among its surface elements, such as word repetitions and reference substitutions[cite: 11].
Patterns of Development
Development is the process by which you support or explain the central idea of a paragraph, essay, or other piece of writing[cite: 11]. Methods include: Narration, Definition, Description, Classification, Illustration, Cause and Effect, and Comparison/Contrast[cite: 11, 12].
Hypertext
Presents a new way to read online text that differs from standard linear reading[cite: 12]. Information is represented in a semantic network where multiple related sections are connected, allowing readers to browse non-linearly by jumping between sections tailored to their background and interests[cite: 12].
Intertext / Intertextuality
Intertextuality is the shaping of a text's meaning by another text through figures such as allusion, quotation, calque, translation, pastiche, and parody[cite: 12]. First introduced by Julia Kristeva, it posits that all texts are constructed as a mosaic of citations, subverting the concept of a text as a self-sufficient totality[cite: 12].
Literature Review
A text written to consider critical points of current knowledge, including substantive findings as well as theoretical and methodological contributions to a particular topic[cite: 13]. As secondary sources, literature reviews do not report original experimental work[cite: 13]. The process involves framing a research question, searching and managing relevant literature, synthesizing research, and writing an assessment[cite: 13, 14].
